One

Bottom line

Neither document is unusual for a private credit lender's opening draft. But as written the package is materially one-sided, and contains at least five provisions that would cause us real harm — plus two that the lender's own attorney has already conceded, in the margins of his own draft, are non-compliant with the National Credit Act.

The three that matter most:

  1. The Sale of Shares proceeds are ceded "out and out" — outright, not as security. Ownership of that claim transfers on signature. There is no clause returning it when the loan is repaid, and no clause obliging Plane Tree to pay back any surplus. And the risk that is easy to miss: on this construction the claim falls into Plane Tree's estate if they become insolvent, leaving Tyron a concurrent creditor for the whole excess. (Pledge cl 4.2, Annexure A.)
  2. There is no ordinary-course-of-business carve-out anywhere in the security document. All book debts and the bank account credit balance are ceded, and cl 6.1 then forbids dealing with any of it without written consent. Read literally, Tyron cannot collect a debtor or draw on his own account from day one.
  3. This is a personal loan with unlimited recourse. No company borrower, no cap. Tyron is personally liable for R2.4m plus interest against his vehicle, plant, receivables, bank account and insurance proceeds — and warrants things no person can truthfully warrant for twelve months.

Separately, and most urgently: the covering email asks for company details for an "AOD/invoice for legal fees." That request should not be answered with company details. See §3.

"21% compounded daily" is not 21% — but argue it under Regulation 40

Must change

Compounded daily, the effective annual rate is 23.36%. Their own attorney twice notes, in comments 8 and 19:

In terms of the national credit act, the maximum interest rate which can be charged is the Repo Rate plus 14%. That is 21% at the moment and we cannot charge 24%.

Their comments 8 and 19

The temptation is to argue "23.36% exceeds the 21% cap". Don't — whether the Regulation 42 cap bites on the nominal or the effective rate is genuinely unresolved. The cleaner point is Regulation 40: interest must be calculated daily but may be added to the deferred amount only once a month. Daily capitalisation is not permitted at all, whatever the headline rate. See §8.

Ask for

Simple interest, or at most monthly capitalisation; and the effective annual rate stated on the face of the agreement alongside the nominal rate.

The "AOD / invoice for legal fees" request

Toivo's email asks us to send "your company details to be used in the AOD/invoice for legal fees." Do not send them. Three reasons, in ascending order of seriousness.

We are probably not liable for these fees at all — and there is binding SCA authority

Loan cl 16 and Pledge cl 9 say the Borrower pays all costs of drafting and implementing the agreements. Their own attorney's comment 49 says otherwise, in terms:

In terms of section 100 of the National Credit Act a credit provider must not charge an amount to the consumer in respect of any fee, charge, commission, expense or other amount payable by the credit provider to any third party… We will need to address this as we cannot charge the consumer the costs for the preparation of this agreement.

Their comment 49

He is right, and the position hardened last year. In National Credit Regulator v National Consumer Tribunal [2025] ZASCA 132 (the "on-the-road fees" case) the SCA held that s101(1) read with s102(1) is a closed list of what a credit provider may require a consumer to pay, and that each charge is examined on its substance, not its label. Drafting fees are not on that list. The initiation fee is the permitted, capped recovery for originating a credit agreement — and Plane Tree has disclosed it as NIL.

They may point to legal fees being recoverable as collection costs. Those are fees incurred in enforcing the consumer's obligations, not in originating the agreement.

The company-invoice route is that attorney's own workaround — flagged as improper

The same comment continues:

We can possibly have his company pay these amounts but it is not strictly permitted.

Their comment 49, continued

The request in Toivo's email is the execution of that workaround. If we supply company details we become a knowing participant in routing a charge around a statutory prohibition — bad for us twice over. We pay money we may not owe, and we create a paper trail showing both parties knew.

In fairness

This route is not automatically fatal. In Uys NO v NCR [2025] ZASCA 34 the SCA held there is nothing unlawful in structuring transactions to avoid the NCA, and a transaction is not simulated merely because it was arranged to avoid regulatory consequences — simulation needs a shared dishonest intention to disguise. If our company genuinely receives a genuine service and genuinely owes the fee, Uys protects it. If it is a pass-through so Tyron bears the cost of drafting his own credit agreement, s100(1) applies. That is a question of fact — which is exactly why we ask rather than assume.

An AOD is a separate, independently enforceable debt — and this exact device has been struck down

An Acknowledgement of Debt is typically a liquid document — it can support provisional sentence or summary judgment, often with a consent to judgment attached. Worse, where an AOD contains a deferral of payment plus interest, fees or charges, it is likely itself a credit agreement, dragging in registration, the closed list and the rate caps.

And in Absa Bank Ltd v Serfontein (SCA, 10 February 2025) an AOD concluded separately from the credit agreement, incorporating a power of attorney permitting execution without court process, was held to be an unlawful supplementary agreement under s91 — unlawful under s90(2)(j), (k) and (a)(i), unseverable, with the agreement declared unlawful and the resulting sale void from the outset. (Citation to be confirmed — see §8.)

Disputes about what we owe are decided by their accountant

Press hardcl 18

Any dispute about the debt, the interest or the rate goes to "the Lender's accountant appointed to the review or audit of its financial statements", whose determination is "final and binding" and reviewable only for "manifest typographical and/or arithmetical error".

That mechanic is probably void. In Ex parte Minister of Justice: In re Nedbank v Abstein Distributors 1995 (3) SA 1 (A) the Appellate Division held that a conclusive-proof clause of which the creditor is the author is per se contrary to public policy and void, because it precludes the debtor from challenging quantum. "The Lender's accountant… final and binding" is the creditor's own instrument producing a binding number. (Distinguish cl 14.4, which says "prima facie proof" — that leaves rebuttal open and on Abstein survives.)

Ask for

An independent expert nominated by SAICA if the parties cannot agree; costs shared; both parties entitled to written representations; manifest error generally, not only arithmetic. The certificate should be signed by an independent auditor, and be proof only for interim relief.

Suspensive conditions run entirely their way

Press hardcl 5
  • 5.1 — conditions must satisfy them within 14 days, "or such longer period as the Lender may, in its sole and absolute discretion, decide". We stay bound indefinitely while they decide.
  • 5.4 — they may waive any condition "with or without notice". We may not know what agreement we are in.
  • 5.3 — our failure is "an Event of Default alternatively this Agreement shall not come into force". Those are mutually exclusive and they pick. You cannot default on an agreement that never came into force.
Ask for

A hard longstop date, either party free to walk away if unfulfilled, waiver on written notice, and "Event of Default" deleted from cl 5.3 — a failed suspensive condition should make the agreement lapse, nothing more.

Press hardcl 8.4

Either this is copy-paste from a suretyship precedent, or it is laying groundwork to treat him as surety for someone else's obligation later. Three smaller ones worth raising

Raise & trade
  • Attorney-and-own-client costs "irrespective of whether legal action results" cl 14.3 — the clause ignores a line the law draws. Pre-litigation recovery costs are "collection costs" (s101(1)(g)), capped and counted toward the s103(5) in duplum ceiling; a pre-agreed cost consent is separately caught by s90(2)(k). Litigation costs sit outside that machinery after Bayport Securitisation v University of Stellenbosch Law Clinic [2021] ZASCA 156 — but an attorney-and-own-client stipulation still does not bind a court. Split the clause: pre-litigation costs "limited to the maximum permitted by ss101 and 103 of the NCA"; litigation costs "such costs as a court may award, on taxation".
  • s129 default notices by registered post cl 13.3 — registered post to an unattended address is still deemed delivery, and starts the clock before they may litigate. Ask for email and post, running from the later.
  • Shortfall risk with no control over the sale cl 1.15 — they may sell by "any other commercially reasonable method determined by the Lender", with no valuation and no reserve, and we carry the shortfall. Ask for an independent valuation, a reserve, and 10 days' notice so we can find a buyer ourselves.
  • "Underlying Transaction" is undefined in substance — yet cl 6.3.4 requires proceeds be applied solely to it, cl 14.1.3 makes any breach a default, and the Pledge uses the same phrase to set the boundary of the security. Schedule the actual transaction.

Five

Pledge and Cession of Claims

The out-and-out cession of the Sale of Shares proceeds

Must changecl 4.2

This is the most damaging clause in the package.

Everything else is ceded in securitatem debiti — as security, ownership stays with Tyron. But cl 4.2 alone cedes the De Jongh / Nel share-sale proceeds "out and out on an outright basis": an absolute transfer of the claim, effective from signature date, not on default.

  • Those proceeds stop being Tyron's asset the moment he signs — before a cent is drawn down.
  • There is no re-cession clause, and that matters more than it looks. SA law recognises two constructions (Grobler v Oosthuizen [2009] ZASCA 51). Under the pledge construction the cedent keeps the reversionary interest and the claim reverts automatically on settlement. Under the out-and-out construction the cessionary takes full title and the cedent has only a personal contractual claim to have it re-ceded. cl 4.9 is enough for the first and useless for the second. Substance can override a label (Cohen's Trustee 1911 AD 235) — but "out and out on an outright basis" is about as clear a contrary intention as can be drafted, so we cannot count on re-characterisation.
  • The risk nobody has flagged: if Plane Tree becomes insolvent, we lose the lot. On the out-and-out construction the ceded claim falls into the cessionary's insolvent estate, and the cedent ranks as a concurrent creditor for its re-cession claim. Under the pledge construction it does not. This is the strongest single reason to insist cl 4.2 changes.
  • There is no surplus-accounting clause for this cession. At common law a cessionary who realises more than the secured debt must refund the excess — but that duty is default, not peremptory, and can be contracted out of, and we found no decided case on an out-and-out cession silent on the point. cl 5.3 does oblige them to credit net proceeds, but it sits in the enforcement clause and speaks to realising security after default; the cl 4.2 cession operates from signature date and sits outside it.
  • We also lose standing to sue the De Jonghs. On Picardi Hotels v Thekweni Properties [2008] ZASCA 128 a security cession deprives the cedent of the right to recover the ceded debt. If Plane Tree is passive and the claim prescribes, Tyron has no direct remedy.
  • cl 4.4 then requires that any payment the De Jonghs make to Tyron be handed over within 48 hours, regardless of whether anything is due.
Ask for — in order of preference
  1. cl 4.2 changed to a cession in securitatem debiti, on the same footing as cl 4.1; or
  2. if they insist on out-and-out, then all three of: an express automatic re-cession on discharge, at their cost within 10 business days; an express obligation to account for and pay over any surplus above the debt within 5 business days; and an express statement that the cession secures only the Secured Obligations.

No ordinary-course carve-out — we cannot run the business

Must changecl 6.1

The Ceded Claims include all present and future book debts and "all of the Borrower's claims against the Borrower's bankers for the net proceeds standing to the credit of the Borrower's Account" — his bank balance. Clause 6.1 then prohibits, without prior written consent:

  • 6.1.2 — selling, transferring, releasing, removing, redirecting or disposing of any Secured Property;
  • 6.1.3 — any action, or omission, that "will, might or tends to in any way adversely affect" their interest;
  • 6.1.6 — "divert, redirect, withdraw, transfer, set off, compromise or otherwise deal with any payment, proceeds, receivable or other amount" other than per "the payment flow approved by the Lender".

There is no ordinary-course permission anywhere in the document, and no payment flow has been agreed or attached. A court would strain to avoid the literal reading. We should never rely on a court straining.

Ask for — non-negotiable

An express clause permitting the Borrower to collect the Ceded Claims and operate the account in the ordinary course, retaining and applying the proceeds, until an Event of Default has occurred and is continuing and written notice invoking the cession has been given. This is standard in every properly drafted security cession; its absence here is conspicuous.

Enforcement "without notice and without any court" — and the NCA is never mentioned

Must changecl 5.1

…immediately enforceable by the Lender in its discretion without the need for prior notice to the Borrower or any prior authorisation from any court or arbitrator.

Pledge cl 5.1

This clause is unenforceable, and probably void. Three independent reasons:

  1. NCA s130(3) overrides it by its own terms — "despite any provision of law or contract to the contrary", a court may determine the matter only if satisfied that s127, s129 or s131 were complied with; s130(4) requires adjournment otherwise. No drafting gets around a provision that expressly anticipates contrary drafting.
  2. Self-help seizure is unconstitutional. In Chief Lesapo v North West Agricultural Bank [1999] ZACC 16 the Constitutional Court struck down a statute letting a creditor attach and sell a defaulting debtor's property on its own authority: taking property from a debtor in possession without a court order is self-help and infringes s34 of the Constitution. All four pledged assets are in Tyron's possession.
  3. It is an unlawful provision under s90. A term dispensing with s129/130/131 defeats the purposes of the Act (s90(2)(a)(i)) and is caught by the s90(2)(k) family. Under s90(3) it is void from inception; under s90(4) a court may sever it or declare the whole agreement unlawful.

And the "the NCA isn't mentioned" gap cuts the opposite way to how it first looks. The words "National Credit Act" and "NCA" appear nowhere in the Pledge, and the Loan Agreement's cl 1 prevalence clause does not reach a separate contract. But NCA s91(a) provides that a credit provider must not induce a consumer to enter a supplementary agreement containing a provision that would be unlawful in a credit agreement. A pledge and cession taken from the same consumer as a condition of the credit is the paradigm supplementary agreement — so the NCA reaches this document whether or not it says so. That was decided on closely comparable facts in Absa Bank Ltd v Serfontein (SCA, 10 Feb 2025).

The argument to make

We should not be frightened by cl 5.1 — it will not do what it says. More usefully, Plane Tree should be more worried about it than we are: on s90(4) an unseverable cluster of unlawful terms in this document (cl 5.1, the cl 6.3 power of attorney, the cl 5.2.9 auditor valuation) could take the entire security package down with it, leaving them unsecured. It is in their own interest to fix.

Ask for — cheap for them to give

One sentence inserted into the Pledge: "This Agreement is subject to the National Credit Act 34 of 2005. To the extent of any conflict between this Agreement and the National Credit Act, or clauses 1 or 19 of the Loan Facility Agreement, the National Credit Act and those clauses shall prevail." That alone neutralises much of the enforcement risk in this document.

Perfection on demand — they can take the operating equipment at any time

Must changecl 4.13cl 4.14

Clause 4.13 obliges us, immediately upon request and with no reference to default, to procure delivery, control, attornment, endorsement and release instructions over the Pledged Property. Clause 4.14 requires original NATIS documents on request.

The Pledged Property is not idle collateral — it is the Amarok, the 1TPH induction furnace, the CAT 416F backhoe loader, the rock hammer and "its inventory from time to time". That is the operating plant. A perfection demand before any default would stop the business, which would then cause a default.

Ask for

Every perfection obligation exercisable only after an Event of Default that is continuing. Before that, we deliver copies of documents, not possession.

They may take our assets into their own name — valued by their own auditor

Press hardcl 5.2.9

Plane Tree may elect to transfer the Secured Property into its own name as beneficial owner at "fair market value". A pactum commissorium — a creditor simply keeping the security on default — is void. But taking the pledge over at a fair price, crediting the debtor with that value and paying over the surplus, is valid: Mapenduka v Ashington 1919 AD 343, and the "quasi-conditional sale" in Graf v Buechel [2003] ZASCA 29, applied in Bock v Duburoro [2003] ZASCA 94. So the structure is sound in principle — the load-bearing element is the fairness of the valuation, exactly what was contested in Bock. And here the valuer, failing agreement in five business days, is "the Lender's auditor"; we pay that auditor's fees; and the same auditor is the cl 18 arbiter of what we owe. The enforcing party chooses the valuer, we fund the valuer, and the number is final. On Abstein reasoning (§4) a binding determination authored by the creditor is vulnerable — and if the fair-value safeguard is illusory, the whole arrangement collapses back into a void pactum commissorium. That is a risk for them as much as us, which makes it a realistic ask.

Ask for

An independent valuer jointly appointed, or nominated by the relevant professional body if agreement fails within 10 business days; costs shared; and a right for us to submit a counter-valuation.

Irrevocable power of attorney to any director of the Lender

Press hardcl 6.3

The Borrower hereby irrevocably appoints any director of the Lender as its agent with power of attorney in rem suam to be its lawful attorney and agent, to sign all such documents and do all such things as may be necessary to give effect to this Agreement.

Pledge cl 6.3

Unlimited in subject matter, unlimited in time, irrevocable, exercisable before any default, held by any director. It would let a Plane Tree director sign documents in Tyron's own name.

This is not merely aggressive — on current SCA authority it is likely unlawful. In Absa Bank Ltd v Serfontein (SCA, 10 Feb 2025) an acknowledgement of debt incorporating a power of attorney, giving the bank an irrevocable ability to execute without recourse to court, fell within s90(2)(k) and s90(2)(j), with the NCA-exclusion element contravening s90(2)(a)(i). The provisions were unseverable; the agreement was declared unlawful and the resulting sale void from the outset. An in rem suam power is by definition irrevocable and in the grantee's own interest, which aggravates rather than cures the problem.

Ask for

Post-default only; confined to specified perfection documents we have already failed to sign within 5 business days of written request; written notice of each exercise.

Two more worth raising

Raise & trade
  • The pledge creates no real security at all — over anything. A pledge of movables requires delivery, and constitutum possessorium is not permitted: Vasco Dry Cleaners v Twycross 1979 (1) SA 603 (A) held that a "pledge" where the article stays with the pledgor creates no real security right. That is true here of the inventory and all four identified assets, which stay in Tyron's possession. This is why cl 4.13 is drafted so aggressively — the delivery demand is the whole security. The constructive counter-offer is a special notarial bond over the four identified assets (Act 57 of 1993), which deems them pledged notwithstanding non-delivery. Two caveats: Ikea Trading v BOE Bank [2004] ZASCA 27 requires assets to be "readily recognisable from the bond alone", so the VIN/serial descriptions must be exact — and fluctuating inventory will not qualify. Offer the four assets; resist the inventory.
  • No representations outside the document — including negligent ones cl 12.4 — kills any claim based on what Plane Tree told us verbally about flexibility on the balloon or refinance. Ask for fraudulent and reckless misrepresentation to be carved out expressly.

Almost every problem above flows from one decision: this is a personal loan to a natural person for what is plainly a business transaction. Nominal or effective?Unresolved. No NCR circular, guidance note or reported decision found either way.

Three things follow — and one is a question for Plane Tree

  • Their attorney has applied the credit facility cap (RR+14% = 21.00%). But a once-off R2.4m secured loan repayable in instalments looks much more like a secured loan / "other credit agreement" (RR+17% = 24.00%) than a credit facility under s8(3). Either the cap reasoning is wrong or the agreement is mis-classified. Worth asking — not to invite a higher rate, but because it tells us how carefully this has been thought through.
  • At 21.00% the headline sits exactly on the credit-facility cap — zero headroom. And the cap is fixed by the repo rate at the time credit is granted, not today's. Had drawdown fallen before 28 May 2026 (repo 6.75%) the cap would have been 20.75% and 21% would already have been over it. Establish the grant date before running the arithmetic.
  • So don't argue the cap — argue Reg 40. Daily capitalisation is impermissible whatever the rate, and the excess is an amount charged in contravention of s100(1)(c). In Loan Company (Pty) Ltd v NCR [2025] ZASCA 40 the SCA measured the contravention by what was actually charged under the Reg 40 mechanics, not by the stated rate. Remedies in that line run to declaring agreements void, ordering refunds, and a s151 fine. This argument does not depend on the unresolved nominal/effective question.
